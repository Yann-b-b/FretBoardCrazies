## 0. Two cross-cutting findings that reorganize the whole gap

### 0.1 Dorian-family extensions (9/11/13) carry NO avoid note — unlike the major/dominant family
On a **minor 7 chord, the natural 11 is a chord tone, not an avoid note**: it sits a **whole
step above the ♭3** (e.g. Dm: F→G is a major 2nd), whereas the major-family avoid-11 rule
exists specifically because the natural 11 sits a **half step above the major 3rd**. The
**Dorian scale itself has no avoid/weak tone** at all. This means **m9, m11, and m13 can be
stacked and used together** with no built-in dissonance rule blocking any of them — a
structurally different situation from maj9/maj7♯11 where the 11 is excluded by rule.
*(jazzguitar.be minor-chords blog, Wikipedia "Avoid note", thejazzpianosite.com "Avoid Notes" —
confirmed 3-0; verified by hand: Eb(♭3 of Dm)→F(natural 11) = major 2nd = 10 semitones, not a
half-step.)*
**Correction of a source error found during this research:** one source (musicogram.com)
claimed the m13 chord's 11th forms "a harsh minor ninth interval with the minor third" — this
is **arithmetically wrong** (Eb→F is a major 2nd/major 9th compound, not a minor 9th) and is
**rejected**; the 11th is omitted from practical m13 voicings for **spacing/muddiness reasons**
(a dense 6-7 note close-voicing problem common to any extended chord), not because of a
harmonic clash rule. *(Own arithmetic — refutes musicogram.com's specific claim.)*

### 0.2 Melodic-minor-family extensions (m(maj7), m6, m6/9) also have no avoid note
The **melodic minor scale's modes have no avoid notes** — "you can play all the notes of the
scale over any of the chords" built from it. This is why m(maj7), m6, and m6/9 (all melodic
minor tonic colors, per the prior survey) tolerate dense voicings freely. *(thejazzpianosite.com
melodic-minor-modes, jazzguitar.be melodic-minor-modes, jazzadvice.com — confirmed 3-0.)*
**Distinguishing detail vs. harmonic minor:** melodic minor's defining feature is the **natural
6 in place of harmonic minor's ♭6** — this is exactly why m(maj7) built from melodic minor
sounds "smoother"/more consonant than one built from harmonic minor (which supplies a ♭6 and a
raised leading tone with an augmented-2nd gap). *(confirmed 2-0, jazz-guitar-licks.com,
thejazzpianosite.com.)*

## 1. m9 / m11 / m13 — the Dorian extensions: same function as m7, not a new function

**Function:** These are **not a new functional category** — they are the plain m7 (ii, or
tonic-minor-i, or subdominant-minor-iv) **with Dorian color notes added**. The function is
carried entirely by root + quality (m7), exactly as established for m7 in the prior surveys;
9/11/13 do not change tonic-vs-ii identity the way m6 does. *(jazzguitar.be minor-chords,
jazzadvice.com — confirmed 3-0.)*

- **As ii (pre-dominant):** `Dm9 – G9 – Cmaj9` and `Dm11 – G13 – Cmaj7` and `Dm11 – G9 – C` are
  cited as *the* standard ii-V-I extension drill — extending the ii chord with 9/11/13 is
  routine and doesn't weaken its pre-dominant pull. *(piano.org ninth-chords, pressbooks.pub
  "9th/11th/13th chords," staytunedguitar.com — confirmed 3-0.)*
- **As tonic-minor-i:** m9/m11/m13 are also standard as the **final resolving chord of a minor
  ii–V–i** (`iiø7 – V7♭9 – im13/im9`), giving "a far richer harmonic texture than a simple minor
  triad." *(jazz-guitar-licks.com m13 page — confirmed 2-0 with pressbooks/piano.org general
  9th/11th/13th framing.)*
- **As subdominant-minor-iv:** extended Dorian colors on the borrowed iv chord are equally
  standard — see §4 (backdoor progression), which explicitly allows Fm6/Fm7/Fm9/Fm11 as the
  "iv minor" chord before ♭VII7. *(jenslarsen.nl "IV minor chords in a major key" — confirmed.)*
- **m11 and quartal/modal vamps:** the canonical modal-jazz minor color is the "So What" voicing
  (E-A-D-G-B over D), which is an **m11 sound built in fourths** (though the literal So What
  voicing omits the 3rd, so it is *m11-implying*, not a literal spelled m11 chord — pitch set is
  9-5-R-11-13 over the bass). In this modal context there is **no resolution at all** — the
  chord simply sustains as a static tonic-color sound (Miles Davis/Bill Evans, *Kind of Blue*).
  *(piano.org "So What Chord," hearandplay.com, jazz-guitar-licks.com quartal-harmony — confirmed
  3-0.)*
- **m13 as ii — one flagged nuance, single-source, not corroborated a 2nd time:** one source
  argues m13 is "more difficult to use as a ii chord because [the 13th] contains the 3rd of the
  dominant chord" (Dm13's 13th = B = the 3rd of the following G7) — i.e. voicing the 13 on the ii
  chord pre-states the incoming V's guide tone and can blunt the ii→V arrival. **STANDARD-
  UNVERIFIED (single source, jenslarsen.nl "The Minor Chord You Never Use") — plausible
  voice-leading reasoning, not found independently corroborated this run.** Treat m13-on-ii as
  functionally fine (§ above) but note this specific voicing caution.

**Chord-scale:** **Dorian** (R-9-♭3-11-5-13-♭7) for all three, no avoid note (§0.1).
**Resolves to / comes from:** identical to whatever the underlying m7 does — ii resolves to V
(dominant, usually altered/♭9 in a minor context); tonic-i is a resting point (comes from
V7♭9/iiø7, goes nowhere required); iv-minor resolves via ♭VII7 backdoor to I (§4).

## 2. m(maj7) — the melodic-minor tonic, and the line-cliché engine (mechanics, not just naming)

**Function:** **Tonic-minor color exclusively** — never a ii or subdominant chord in the
surveyed sources. It is "the tonic chord of melodic minor" (already established in survey 2) and
also arises as **i in harmonized harmonic minor** (1-♭3-5-7 of harmonic minor is also
m(maj7)) — so it's reachable from either minor parent scale. *(Wikipedia "Minor major seventh
chord," jazzguitar.be minor-major-seventh-chords — confirmed 3-0.)*

- **What precedes/follows it:** as a *static* tonic color it simply sits on i, generally
  preceded by V7♭9/V7alt (minor ii–V) and not required to move anywhere — its major-7th is "more
  tension than any other minor-family chord" (survey 3) and is usually **not held as a long
  target tone**; it moves as part of the descending inner line (below) or is followed by m7/m6
  as a color shift on the same root. *(jazz-guitar-licks.com, antonjazz.com — confirmed 2-0.)*
- **The minor-line-cliché, mechanically (filling the gap survey 3 left as "basic mechanics
  only"):** the descent is **i → i(maj7) → i7 → i6**, a chromatic *inner voice* stepping root →
  major-7 → ♭7 → 6 while the m triad stays fixed above/below it — i.e. m(maj7) is not an
  independent destination but **one frame of a single continuously-falling voice**. The classic
  instance (*My Funny Valentine*, Rodgers & Hart) states it three times at the tune's opening;
  the descent's final note (6̂, i.e. Dm6's A in the D-minor instance) commonly **routes into the
  3rd of the following V7/V9** (Dm–DmMaj7–Dm7–G9/B), turning the cliché into a connective
  voice-leading bridge to the next dominant rather than a self-contained tonic gesture.
  *(Confirms survey-3's flagged-unverified claim — now corroborated by antonjazz.com "Minor
  Major" + jazzguitar.be minor-major-seventh-chords + pianowithjonny.com "spy chord," 3-0. This
  closes survey 3's open UNVERIFIED flag on the cliché.)*
- **The "James Bond chord" as a concrete, sourced instance:** the Bond theme itself moves
  **Em – Em(♭6) – Em6 – Em**, i.e. a *partial* line-cliché fragment (triad → ♭6-color →
  natural-6 → triad) rather than the full i-i(maj7)-i7-i6 descent — cited across three
  independent pages as "the James Bond chord," commonly analyzed as **Em(maj9)** at the point of
  maximum color. Its character is explicitly described as "unresolved" — i.e. this is a
  **deliberately non-cadential, sustained-tension deployment**, not a chord that resolves
  anywhere in the excerpt. *(jazzguitar.be "James Bond Theme," pianowithjonny.com, The James Bond
  Dossier — confirmed 3-0.)*

**Chord-scale:** melodic minor (ascending) primarily; harmonic minor also cited as usable
(different color: ♭6 + raised 7 vs. melodic minor's natural 6 + raised 7). No avoid note (§0.2).
**Resolves to / comes from:** functions as a **decoration of tonic-minor i**, typically
sandwiched between the minor triad and m7/m6 as part of the line cliché; comes from V7♭9/iiø7
like any minor tonic; does not resolve *outward* on its own — its tension is resolved by the
continuing descent, not by a chord change.

## 3. maj9 — plain tonic color, AND a legitimate IV/subdominant color (dual-function, degree-dependent)

**Function:** maj9 is **not fixed to one function** — sources place it at **both I (tonic)** and
**IV (subdominant)** depending on scale degree, unlike m6 (tonic-only) or m7-on-ii (pre-dominant
only). Its function is purely a matter of **which scale degree it's built on**, not an inherent
property of the maj9 quality itself. *(thejazzpianosite.com "Harmonic Functionality,"
jazzguitarlessons.net "Ultimate Guide to Jazz Harmony" — confirmed 2-0 for the I case; general
IV-chord-extension framing for the IV case is textbook-standard but drew only generic/secondary
sourcing this run — **STANDARD-UNVERIFIED** for the IV-specific claim, though the underlying
"IV takes 9ths/13ths" fact is uncontroversial diatonic extension.)*

- **As I (tonic):** `Dm9 – G9 – Cmaj9` — the standard ii-V-I extension pattern already verified
  in §1 doubles as maj9's tonic arrival; maj9 **omits the avoid-11** exactly as the earlier
  major-tonic survey established (no new claim here, just confirms maj9 obeys that rule). Comes
  from V (dominant, often extended G9/G13); goes nowhere further — it's a resting tonic.
- **As IV:** builds on the 4th scale degree (e.g. Fmaj9 in C), functioning as **pre-dominant/
  subdominant**, "a transitional chord between the first and fifth degrees" en route to V. This
  differs functionally from I-maj9 despite identical chord spelling relative to its own root —
  **the same quality, two different functional slots, decided by scale degree alone.**

**Chord-scale:** Ionian (major scale) for I-maj9; Lydian is also commonly substituted for
IV-maj9 specifically because the raised 4th/♯11 removes the avoid-11 that would otherwise sit a
half-step above IV's own 3rd (this is the same avoid-11 logic from survey 3, now explicitly
applied to the IV scale-degree case). Avoid note: natural 11 (same rule as any major-family
chord, per survey 3) — this is why IV commonly upgrades to maj7♯11/Lydian rather than staying
plain maj9 when the 4th-degree tension matters melodically (see §4 below).

## 4. maj7♯11 — Lydian color: tonic substitute AND IV chord (confirmed dual use, no fixed resolution)

**Function:** Two independently-sourced functions, both confirmed, and the sources explicitly
decline to rank one as primary:
1. **IV chord (Lydian-on-IV):** "usually played on a IVmaj7" — the ♯11 is simply IV's diatonic
   raised-4th color relative to the tonic major scale (in C major, F Lydian's B natural = C's
   own scale, i.e. **playing IV with a ♯11 uses the notes of the home major scale**, not a
   borrowed mode). *(jazzguitar.be major-chords, jazz-guitar-licks.com Lydian-mode — confirmed
   3-0.)*
2. **Tonic substitute / ending color:** "Lydian chords may function as subdominants **or
   substitutes for the tonic** in major keys," explicitly cited as good for **ending a tune with
   a bit of tension** — an "ethereal rest" (survey 3's phrase, now corroborated independently by
   Wikipedia's "Lydian chord" article) rather than a chord requiring further resolution.
   *(Wikipedia "Lydian chord," jazzimproviser.com — confirmed 2-0.)*

**What it resolves to / comes from — genuinely context-dependent, stated explicitly:** sources
do **not** give maj7♯11 a fixed resolution target; when used on IV it's reached from I and moves
toward V/I like any subdominant; when used as a tonic substitute it is a **destination**, not a
resolving chord. **This is a case where the sources themselves say function is contextual rather
than fixed — flagging per this survey's instructions rather than asserting one resolution.**
*(confirmed 2-0 that both uses exist; the "which one in which context" beyond degree-placement
is not further disambiguated by the sources found.)*
One usage note: within a ii-V-I in C, a maj7♯11 tonic arrival can be **approached by chromatically
targeting the 3rd of the V chord** from the ii-V line — a melodic/voice-leading device, not a
harmonic-function claim. *(jazzguitar.be major-chords blog — single-source, STANDARD-UNVERIFIED
as stated but low-stakes.)*

**Chord-scale:** Lydian (R-9-3-♯11-5-13-7). **No avoid note** — this is Lydian's defining
property: raising the 4th to ♯11 is specifically what *removes* the major-scale avoid-11.
*(Wikipedia "Lydian chord," confirmed via survey 3's already-established Lydian-has-no-avoid-note
finding — reconfirmed here independently.)*

## 5. 6/9 and m6/9 — tonic-only colors, now with historical/mechanical grounding

**Function:** **Tonic-only**, confirming and sharpening survey 3's finding. New this round: a
**peer-reviewed historical/theoretical source** (Martin, *Music Theory Online* 29.2, "On the
Tonic Added-Sixth Chord in Jazz") gives the added-6th chord (the ancestor of 6/9) real
grounding:
- **Historical origin (CONFIRMED, primary/peer-reviewed):** "By the late 1920s and especially
  the early 1930s...the added sixth became the tonic chord of choice in jazz," predating and then
  coexisting with the bebop-era tonic-maj7. Martin treats added-6 and maj7 tonics as **"distinct
  branches" of tonic harmony**, not simply interchangeable notations of "the same chord" — a
  useful nuance beyond the earlier surveys' "6/9 is more stable than maj7" claim. *(Martin, MTO
  29.2 — confirmed, peer-reviewed primary source.)*
- **Structural reason the 5th matters (CONFIRMED):** unlike 7th chords (where the 5th is
  omittable), "the [added-]sixth chord requires the presence of the fifth to make its harmonic
  function clear" — i.e. a 6/9 voicing that drops the 5th risks losing its tonic identity
  (readable instead as a different chord/inversion). *(Martin, MTO 29.2 — confirmed.)*
- **Voice-leading behavior of the added 6th (CONFIRMED):** Martin distinguishes a **"dependent"**
  6th (one that resolves melodically down to the more stable 5th of the scale) from an
  **"independent"** 6th (one that doesn't depend on resolving to the 5th, standing as a stable
  color tone in its own right) — independent-6th usage becomes increasingly the jazz-standard
  treatment. This gives the engine a concrete voice-leading behavior for the 6th scale degree
  itself, not just a chord-level label. *(Martin, MTO 29.2 — confirmed, primary/peer-reviewed.)*
- **Scope limit, stated explicitly by the source itself:** Martin's article **explicitly excludes
  6/9 and minor tonics from its scope** ("discussions of tonic 6-9 chords…are outside the scope
  of this article"; minor tonics receive no discussion at all) — so the peer-reviewed grounding
  applies to the **plain added-6th**, and its extension to 6/9/m6/9 specifically is this survey's
  inference by analogy, **not** itself peer-reviewed. Flagging this explicitly per the
  adversarial-verification requirement.
- **m6/9 specifically:** remains sourced only at the pedagogical-blog tier (jazz-guitar-licks.com
  6/9-chords page: "Cmin6/9 = C Eb G A D," "works well on the I chord or in ii-V-I progressions
  to make the harmony more colorful") — **STANDARD-UNVERIFIED at peer-reviewed tier**, though
  consistent with the established m6-tonic finding from survey 3 (m6/9 is m6 + the 9, same
  tonic-only function, no new mechanism found this round).

**Chord-scale:** major 6/9 → major scale (Ionian) or the **major-6th-diminished scale** (already
covered in survey 2, Barry Harris); m6/9 → **Dorian or melodic minor** (both supply a natural 6th
— Aeolian, with its ♭6, cannot generate this chord). No avoid note in either case (§0.1/0.2 — the
6/9 dyad and the Dorian/melodic-minor extensions are mutually avoid-note-free).
**Resolves to / comes from:** as established, these are **terminal/resting tonic colors** —
typically the *last* chord of a turnaround or tune (more stable than maj7/m7 as an ending,
survey 3), reached from V (dominant) or iiø7-V7♭9 (minor), and not themselves requiring further
movement.

## What this gives the engine

1. **New suggestion-graph edges (gap qualities):**
   - `m7(ii) ↔ m9(ii) ↔ m11(ii) ↔ m13(ii)` — same function, freely interchangeable extension tier
     (no function change, unlike m6).
   - `m7(iv-minor) ↔ m9/m11(iv-minor) → ♭VII7 → I` — the backdoor family, all Dorian-extension
     tolerant.
   - `i(triad) → i(maj7) → i7 → i6 → [3rd of V7]` — the line-cliché as a literal edge-chain, not
     just a label; **this is the one deployment pattern that is a genuine forced sequence rather
     than a free-choice color swap**, useful for a "next chord" suggestion when m(maj7) is
     active.
   - `IV(maj9) ↔ IV(maj7♯11)` and `I(maj7♯11) as terminal` — maj7♯11 gets **two** graph roles
     depending on whether it's tagged IV-position or terminal-tonic-position; the engine needs a
     position tag, not just a quality tag, to route this correctly.
   - `I(6/9) / i(m6/9)` — confirmed terminal-only nodes with no outbound edge required (resting
     states), consistent with 6 as tonic-only from survey 3.
2. **A structural rule to encode, not just a lookup table:** for the **Dorian-extension family
   (m9/m11/m13)**, function is inherited entirely from the root m7 — the engine should **not**
   create separate function labels per extension the way it must for m6 vs m7. This simplifies
   the minor-color state machine: one function slot ("m7-family"), with 9/11/13 as
   freely-combinable color flags on it.
2b. **maj9 needs a position tag (I vs IV), not a fixed function** — same quality, two slots.
3. **A caution to surface, not enforce:** m13-on-ii voicing (natural 13 pre-stating the V's 3rd)
   is a single-sourced stylistic caution, not a rule — the engine should **allow** m13 as ii but
   could optionally flag it as "advanced/context-sensitive" rather than blocking it.
4. **A corrected fact:** m11/m13 have **no** internal avoid-note dissonance from the 11
   itself (unlike the major/dominant family) — do not port the major-family avoid-11 rule onto
   minor qualities in the engine's avoid-note table.
