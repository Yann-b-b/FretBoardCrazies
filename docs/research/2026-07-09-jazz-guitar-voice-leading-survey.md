# Jazz Guitar Voice-Leading & Chord-Shape Transitions — Theory Survey

**Date:** 2026-07-09
**Method:** deep-research harness — 5 search angles, 20 sources fetched, 85 claims
extracted, top 25 adversarially verified (3-vote), 24 confirmed / 1 refuted, synthesized
to 9 high-confidence findings.
**Scope:** landscape/theory survey (jazz), not app-implementation recommendations.

---

## The unifying principle

**Good voice leading = least possible movement.** Across every source, the single
organizing idea is that you connect chords by moving each voice the shortest distance to
the next chord — common tone held > step motion > leap. On guitar this becomes a concrete
rule: **find the closest available next chord *shape* rather than jumping between distant
shapes.** This one principle ties together both axes below — the within-position
guide-tone/inversion choices and the across-neck CAGED "nearest grip" choice are the same
idea expressed at different scales. *(jazzguitarlessons.net; Open Music Theory,
viva.pressbooks.pub — confirmed 3-0.)*

## Axis 1 — Within a position (voice leading between nearby grips)

### Guide tones: the 3rd and the 7th
The **3rd and 7th are the "guide tones."** They alone determine chord quality: the **3rd**
distinguishes major vs. minor, the **7th** distinguishes maj7 / m7 / dominant. Playing only
these two notes conveys a progression, because they resolve by half-step through ii–V–I. A
retained **common tone** can change harmonic function as the chord moves under it (a held
pitch being the root of one chord, the 5th of another, the 3rd of a third). *(jazzguitar.be;
fundamental-changes.com; musictheory.pugetsound.edu — a university theory text;
learnjazzstandards.com — confirmed 3-0.)*
**Scope note:** the 3rd/7th framework describes maj/min/dom — **not** sus, dim7, or aug.

### The ii–V–I motion, mechanically
ii7–V7–IM7 is **the most common progression in jazz and the first one taught in most method
books** (McClimon, *Music Theory Online* — the one peer-reviewed source that survived
verification). Its voice leading: root moves down a fifth, **the 7th of one chord falls a
half-step to become the 3rd of the next**, and the old 3rd becomes the new 7th (a common
tone). Concretely, Dm7 (D-F-A-C) → G7 (G-B-D-F): the 7th **C falls to B** (3rd of G7) while
**F is retained** as the 7th of G7 — so a *single voice* can move yet the m7→dominant change
is clearly heard. *(mtosmt.org; jazzguitarlessons.net — confirmed 3-0.)*

### Shell voicings
The **shell voicing = root + 3rd + 7th (omit the 5th)**, a sparse three-note grip. Because
it's just the root plus the two guide tones, it exposes the harmonic connection between
chords directly and leaves fingers/thumb free (useful for walking-bass comping). It's an
equally standard comping vehicle alongside drop-2/drop-3; the choice is largely preference.
*(weissguitar.com; jazzguitar.be walking-bass — supporting.)*

### Drop-2 and drop-3 voicings
A **drop-2 voicing** is a four-note close-position seventh chord with its **second-highest
voice dropped an octave** (numbering voices top-down), producing an open, playable grip —
e.g. close Cmaj7 top-down is B-G-E-C; dropping the 2nd-from-top (G) an octave yields
**G-C-E-B**. **Drop-3** drops the *third*-highest voice. *(jazzguitar.be;
learnjazzstandards.com; jenslarsen.nl; jazz-guitar-licks.com — confirmed 3-0.)*

- **Why they dominate on guitar:** close-position four-note chords are physically
  unplayable in standard (fourths-based) tuning; dropping a voice widens the outer interval
  and spreads the notes across **four adjacent strings**. A 6-string guitar has exactly
  three adjacent four-string sets — **6543, 5432, 4321** — and drop-2/drop-3 shapes live on
  these. *(confirmed 3-0.)*
- **How you comp a ii–V–I with them:** you **select inversions** so that two notes move and
  two stay per change (rather than holding one inversion and leaping), typically descending
  the neck through inversions with minimal motion. *(confirmed 3-0.)* Caveat: sources call
  this *a* primary vehicle, not the only one — shell/guide-tone voicings are equally standard.

## Axis 2 — Across the neck (connecting shapes up and down the fretboard)

### The CAGED system
Five movable shapes recur in a **fixed cyclic order C → A → G → E → D → C** up the neck,
each connecting to its neighbor via a shared root, giving **the same chord in multiple
locations**. The practical payoff for voice leading: with several positions available for
each chord, a player can **pick the grip closest to the previous one** for economical
transitions — the across-neck expression of "closest next shape." The entry point depends on
the key, but the order never changes. *(jazzguitarguide.com; cagedguitarlessons.com;
D'Addario, Applied Guitar Theory via verifiers — confirmed 3-0.)*
**⚠️ Refuted sub-claim:** one source's specific per-shape *root-string* mapping (C = 2nd-string
root, A = 5th, G = 3rd, E = 6th, D = 4th) **failed verification (1-2)** and should be treated
as unreliable — the C-A-G-E-D *cyclic order* and *multiple-positions* facts are solid, but
that exact per-shape string assignment is disputed.

## Harmonic extension — tritone substitution
**Tritone sub** replaces a dominant with another dominant a **tritone away**; the two share
the same **guide-tone (3rd/7th) tritone pair enharmonically**, with 3rd and 7th swapping
roles — G7 (B/F) ≡ Db7 (F/Cb=B). The substitute root **descends by half-step** into
resolution (Db7 → C), giving smoother **chromatic bass motion** than the V–I leap; in
Dm7-G7-Cmaj7 it produces Dm7-Db7-Cmaj7 with a D-Db-C bass line. Standard in Levine's *Jazz
Theory Book* and Berklee pedagogy. *(jazzguitar.be; learnjazzstandards.com — confirmed 3-0.)*

## Caveats on this survey

- **Source quality skews to reputable teaching blogs** (jazzguitar.be, learnjazzstandards.com,
  jenslarsen.nl, fundamental-changes.com, jazzguitarlessons.net) rather than the canonical
  named texts. **Only one primary/peer-reviewed source** survived (McClimon, *MTO*).
- **None of the named authors/systems produced a directly-sourced surviving claim** — Levine,
  Ted Greene, Mick Goodrick (*The Advancing Guitarist*), Jimmy Bruno, the Barry Harris method,
  and Berklee methodology are referenced only secondhand. This is acceptable because every
  surviving claim is textbook-stable, independently derivable theory (verifiable by
  note-spelling), but the survey does **not** directly document those pedagogies' distinctive
  contributions.
- **Mild overstatements folded in:** "drop-2/3 are THE comping vehicle" and "voice leading =
  closest shape" lean toward exclusivity; shell voicings and tendency-tone/parallel-avoidance
  nuance are equally standard and under-covered.
- Voice-leading theory is stable — **no staleness risk.**

## Open questions (candidates for a follow-up research round)

1. **The named pedagogies' distinctive contributions** — Barry Harris (sixth-diminished
   scale, "borrowing"/scale-of-chords), Ted Greene (*Chord Chemistry*, the V-system), Mick
   Goodrick (systematic voicing generation), Jimmy Bruno — none produced a sourced claim here.
2. **How the harder extended/altered qualities move to one another** — 9ths, 11ths, 13ths,
   altered dominants, m7♭5, dim7, 6/9, sus. The survivors cover only the maj7/m7/dom guide-tone
   framework; chord-scale relationships and altered voice leading are under-documented.
3. **Shell vs. drop-2/drop-3 as systems** — when players choose one over the other (sources
   said "preference" but didn't systematize the tradeoff).
4. **Applying this to *generating* voicing sequences when improvising** (chord melody /
   chordal soloing), as distinct from comping — the evidence here is heavily comping-oriented.
